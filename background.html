WHY FOOD ACCESS MATTERS

Food Wellness, Food Deserts & Public Health

Understanding how nutrition shapes physical and mental wellbeing, the systemic factors behind food deserts, and the public health programs working to expand equitable access to fresh food.

Explore the research and public health context behind our directory, from the role of daily nutrition in preventing chronic illness to the neighborhood barriers that limit fresh food access.

01

Importance of Food Wellness

Physical Health, Mental Wellbeing & Nutrition

Repeated studies have agreed that food is a very important aspect in someone’s health, and what one eats can have a big impact on both their physical and mental wellbeing. Proper food nutrition is required for growth and brain development, disease prevention, and good mood.

For example, heart health has been directly linked to one’s diet. Tailoring your diet by eating foods like vegetables, fruits, and whole grains can help reduce blood pressure and help prevent or slow heart disease. Eating healthy has also been connected to a reduced cancer risk and improved mood due to impacts on the gut microbiome, blood glucose levels, and immune activation.

According to the CDC, fewer than 1 in 10 children and adults eat the recommended amount of vegetables. Many things may factor into this statistic, with one of them being food deserts, which our organization focuses on battling.

02

What are Food Deserts

Neighborhood Barriers & Systemic Contributing Factors

Food deserts are communities that have poor access to healthy and affordable foods. They tend to be in low-income and historically marginalized areas throughout the country. Food deserts make it harder to access healthy foods because things like fruits, vegetables, whole grains, dairy, peas, beans, meat, and fish are expensive to purchase or simply not an available option at all.

Contributing Factors to Food Deserts
02.1

Food Insecurity

Food insecurity is the disrupted or limited access to sufficient nutrients at all times. It can be temporary (for example, running out of food for a few days) or long term (such as persistent poverty) and is 2.5 times greater in low-income areas than the national average.

02.2

Social Determinants of Health

Social determinants of health are factors beyond one’s control that impact their ability to access food. They can be caused by income, community infrastructure, and access to supermarkets, alongside other factors like education, employment, socioeconomic status, and access to healthcare and fresh produce. All of these can contribute to health disparities within food deserts, such as increased chronic illnesses.

02.3

Racial Segregation

Low-income communities have a higher proportion of Black and Latino people who are disproportionately impacted by limited food access. Decades of systemic racism and segregation have placed historically marginalized populations in impoverished neighborhoods. For example, in 2016, Black households were two times likelier to be food insecure while Hispanic households had an overall greater presence in food insecurity—and this problem continues to this day, a decade later.

02.4

Lack of Accessible Transport

People in food deserts may have to travel further distances to get to supermarkets. Predominantly Black or Hispanic communities tend to have fewer supermarkets and local food markets (which are important for offering nutritious produce) compared to white communities. Another factor is that public transport and vehicle ownership can impact how easy it is to access food.

03

How Health Systems Currently Support Food Wellness

Public Health Infrastructure & CDC Nutrition Initiatives

Many players are involved in the regulation and management of food throughout the country, including the government, insurance, growers, food retailers, community members, researchers, and more. It is a very complex system that has to go through many hoops to alleviate systemic issues present in the U.S. and other countries across the globe.

There are multiple CDC programs that are dedicated to relieving the stress citizens face in terms of food insecurity:

CDC Nutrition & Food Access Programs
03.1

State Physical Activity and Nutrition (SPAN)

Focuses on poor nutrition, physical inactivity, and obesity by promoting food services and nutrition guidelines, expanding fruit and vegetable voucher incentives and produce prescription programs, promoting safe and accessible physical activity, supporting the continuation of breastfeeding for infants, and bolstering early education on activity and nutrition.

03.2

Racial and Ethnic Approaches to Community Health (REACH)

Seeks to reduce racial and ethnic health disparities using culturally appropriate programs for a wide variety of groups. It prioritizes improving health, preventing chronic diseases (such as heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and obesity), and reducing disparity.

03.3

High Obesity Program (HOP)

Focuses on rural areas in the U.S. where at least 40% of adult residents have obesity by expanding fruit and vegetable voucher incentives and produce, increasing physical activity through community design, creating family healthy-weight programs, and promoting early education.

03.4

Strategies for Fruit and Vegetable Voucher Incentives and Produce Prescriptions

Focuses on groups that may have difficulty with access to healthy fruits and vegetables, such as people living in rural areas, tribal communities, neighborhoods with lower incomes, and people with disabilities. Fruit and vegetable incentives are coupons or cash incentives that consumers can use at the point of purchase, while produce prescriptions are prescriptions for fruits and vegetables to be used in a healthcare setting or in the patient’s community.
